Third companion · the evidence apparatus

Proving Ground

For a system whose entire deliverable is defensible measured claims, the test apparatus is not quality assurance sitting beside the work — it is the work. This is the design for it: seven layers, what each one can and cannot establish, which parts already exist and should be taken rather than written, and the one property that must be designed into F from the first commit because it can never be retrofitted.

01

The reframe

Section 13 of the architecture document says the question that replaces "who are your first thousand users" is whether you can state a small number of claims, each with a named baseline and a number, that a competent skeptic could try to refute and fail. Everything in this document follows from taking that literally.

Three consequences that are easy to nod at and hard to actually accept:

  • An unmeasured improvement did not happen. Not "is probably fine" — did not happen. If a design decision cannot be tied to a number that moves, it is a preference.
  • An unreproducible measurement is an anecdote. Including one you personally watched.
  • The apparatus is on the critical path, not beside it. A system that ships its architecture first and its measurement second will discover, two years in, that half its claims were never instrumentable and the other half regressed silently somewhere it cannot now find.
The load-bearing consequence One property — whole-system determinism — has to be designed into F from the first commit. Every other layer here can be added later at ordinary cost. Determinism cannot: retrofitting it means finding and re-plumbing every source of nondeterminism in a system that has grown around their absence, which is why no mainstream kernel has it and why none of them will ever get it.
02

Why F can do what Linux cannot

Deterministic simulation testing is the strongest known technique in systems reliability. FoundationDB built a single-threaded simulator that runs an entire cluster — simulated disks, networks, machine crashes — deterministically, and has accumulated on the order of a trillion CPU-hours of simulated testing. TigerBeetle's simulator reproduces any bug exactly from a seed plus a commit hash, and reports that a minute of simulated time is worth days of real-world testing. The technique is well understood and the tooling is maturing fast.

It is also almost never applied to operating systems, because operating systems are the hardest possible target: nondeterminism enters through interrupts, timers, DMA, core scheduling, device responses, and ambient global state, at thousands of unmarked places.

F's architecture removes almost all of them — not deliberately, but as a side effect of decisions already made for other reasons.

Architectural decisionWhy it was madeWhat it hands the simulator

A research claim in its own right An operating system architected so that whole-system deterministic simulation is tractable. This is not a testing convenience — it is an argument that the same properties which make a system fast and safe also make it verifiable by simulation, and that the three are the same design pressure viewed from different angles. Nobody has demonstrated this at OS scale. It is recorded as a claim in architecture section 13, and as the seventh entry in the novelty list in Lineage and Debts section 07.

04

Layer 0 — the determinism substrate

The rule is absolute and it constrains F's own design, not just the test harness: nothing in the system may observe nondeterminism except through a capability that the simulator can substitute.

That means time is not an instruction, it is a capability. Randomness is a capability. Interrupt arrival, ring consumer ordering, device response latency, core assignment and memory-allocation addresses are all decided by an injectable policy that is the real scheduler in production and a seeded deterministic one under test.

What "no ambient nondeterminism" means in practice
// FORBIDDEN anywhere in the system, including tests and drivers:
rdtsc() · clock_gettime() · rdrand() · HashMap::new() // random seed
address-dependent iteration · unsynchronized wall-clock reads

// REQUIRED — all of it substitutable by the simulator:
trait Env {
    fn now(&self) -> Instant;          // virtual clock under test
    fn rng(&self) -> &mut dyn Rng;        // seeded
    fn sched(&self) -> &mut dyn Scheduler; // decides every interleaving
}

// The contract that makes everything else possible:
//   (seed, commit_hash) → byte-identical execution, always.
Enforce it mechanically on day one. A lint that fails the build on any forbidden call, applied to the whole tree including imported code paths. This costs an afternoon at milestone M0 and is effectively impossible to impose at month eighteen, because by then the violations are load-bearing and each one has a reason. The discipline is the deliverable here — the substrate is only a few hundred lines.

One deliberate consequence worth stating: this makes hash-map iteration order, allocator addresses and thread interleaving reproducible, which eliminates the entire category of bugs that appear once in ten thousand runs and can never be reproduced by the person who has to fix them. That category is where kernel engineering time actually goes.

05

Layer 1 — deterministic simulation

With layer 0 in place, the system runs single-threaded inside a simulator that controls every decision. The simulator supplies modelled devices and injects faults; a seed selects the whole trajectory.

The system, and not the frame. This section said the entire system until RFC 0032 reversed it. What f-sim models is everything above the frame — components, the rings between them, and the devices at the far end of those rings — and it does not build, link or execute any part of kernel/. The reason is not a shortcut: a host-buildable frame needs a mocked architecture layer, that mock is a second machine nobody checks against the first, and the parts it would replace — paging, the APIC, the multiboot map, the VT-d unit — are exactly the parts most likely to be wrong. A green simulation of a kernel running on a machine nobody built is the shape of evidence this project exists to refuse.

So the cost is stated rather than absorbed: the simulator will never catch a bug inside the frame's own algorithms. A defect in the buddy allocator's coalescing pass, in the capability table's revocation walk or in the page-table walker is invisible from here, and what covers those is named elsewhere in this document — the boot suite (cargo xtask run, orders, user, cap, iommu), the mutation harness (cargo xtask mutate, RFC 0017), and the bounded proofs at layer 3 that E1-P07 and E1-P12 land. A reader who wants to know what checks the frame should look there and will find it, rather than looking here and finding a simulator that appears to cover it.

The consequence for the phrase whole boot-to-workload run is a pair rather than one process, and RFC 0035 and RFC 0036 are where the pair is defined and where the gap between its two halves is declared: cargo xtask trace --hash boots the real kernel in QEMU and hashes that, cargo xtask sim --hash deployment runs the compiled component set and hashes that, and cargo xtask sim --join is what requires the two to be about the same components.

What to model, and what to break

Two refinements worth adopting from current practice rather than rediscovering:

  • Protocol-aware fault injection. Uniformly random faults spend most of their budget on uninteresting states. Targeting faults at protocol state transitions — the moment a capability is revoked, the instant a ring epoch increments, the window between claim and publish — finds far more per unit of simulated time. TigerBeetle published this refinement in 2026 and it is directly applicable to F's ring and capability protocols.
  • Seed sweeps as the CI workload. Continuous background seed exploration, with any failing seed captured as a permanent regression test. A failing seed is the bug report: it reproduces byte-identically on any machine at that commit, which converts the hardest class of kernel bug into an ordinary one.
What this layer establishes: that the logic of the system above the frame is correct under adversarial ordering and hardware failure. Two things it cannot establish, and both matter: anything about real timing, real hardware behaviour or real performance — the simulator's clock is fictional, which is why layers 5 and 6 exist, and conflating the two is the most common way simulation-tested systems still ship latency bugs — and anything about the frame's own algorithms, which run in QEMU under the boot suite and the mutation harness and are proved rather than sampled at layer 3. RFC 0032.
06

Layer 2 — concurrency and the memory model

Deterministic simulation explores orderings the scheduler chooses. It does not explore what a weak memory model permits — the reorderings the hardware itself is allowed to perform. For F that gap sits directly on the most critical code in the system.

The ring's correctness rests entirely on one release-store and one acquire-load. The implementation companion already flags the trap: using Relaxed there works on x86-64 and fails on ARM64, which is the worst available failure mode because every test on an x86 laptop passes. Simulation will not catch it. Only a memory-model tool will.

ToolWhat it doesUse it for
RustMCon GenMCStateless model checking of unmodified concurrent Rust, via LLVM IR, under axiomatic weak memory models including RC11.The primary tool. Verify the ring cursor protocol, the epoch mechanism and the multi-producer claim under weak memory rather than under an assumed ordering.
LoomexhaustiveExhaustive permutation of interleavings for small concurrent Rust units.Small, high-value primitives where exhaustiveness is affordable and worth having.
ShuttlerandomizedRandomized scheduling, trading soundness for scale.Larger subsystems where exhaustive checking will not terminate.
Litmus testshand-writtenMinimal programs whose outcome distinguishes one ordering from another.Regression guards. A test that must fail under Relaxed and pass under Release/Acquire is what stops a future contributor from "optimizing" the ordering away.
Run the whole suite on both architectures, always. x86-64's total-store-order model hides the entire class of bug this layer exists to find. An ARM64 target in CI is not a portability nicety for F — it is the only configuration where these tests mean anything.
07

Layer 3 — proof, deliberately targeted

Architecture section 08 already sets the policy: verify the frame's invariants and the ring protocols, not the system. This layer is how, and the honest cost.

The tooling has moved. Verus is the current state of the art for Rust, embedding specifications and proofs in Rust syntax with linear ghost types and an SMT backend; case studies report roughly five lines of proof per line of implementation, which is exactly why the target must stay small. Kani is the lighter complement — bounded model checking needing only contracts and harnesses, no proof code — and is the right tool for validation and parsing paths where bounded exhaustiveness is enough.

The development worth knowing about AI-assisted proof generation has begun landing on real kernel code. KVerus produced upstream-accepted Verus proofs for 23 previously unverified functions in Asterinas's memory-management module — and Asterinas is F's closest kernel relative, which makes both the technique and the proof idioms directly transferable. Reported success is around 80% on single-file tasks and 51% at repository level with cross-file dependencies. That is not "proof is now free," but it materially changes the cost of the frame's proof obligation, which bet 01 identifies as the thing most likely to sink the kernel design.

What to actually prove, in priority order

  • Capability soundness. Derivation only ever weakens rights; revocation is complete through the derivation tree; no sequence of operations yields authority that was not granted. This is bet 04's entire content, and if it is wrong nothing else in the system matters.
  • The frame's unsafe API obligations. Bet 01 concedes that frame soundness is a proof obligation rather than a compiler guarantee. Discharge it explicitly, or the framekernel's safety argument is an assertion.
  • Address-space and mapping invariants. The Asterinas precedent means there is now prior art to work from rather than starting cold.
  • Ring protocol state machines. Bounded checking with Kani is enough here — the state space is small and the properties are simple. Full deductive proof is not worth the ratio.
08

Layer 4 — fuzzing the one surface

F has an unusual advantage here. Linux presents hundreds of syscalls and an unbounded ioctl surface, which is why syzkaller needs a whole description language to generate meaningful programs. F presents one interface: the ring. Every interaction with every service is a stream of submission entries. That is a dramatically smaller and better-structured target.

  • Structure-aware submission generation. Generate valid-shaped SQE streams from the opcode schemas, with the capability field, buffer references and chaining flags mutated within their type constraints. The equivalent of syzkaller's description language, but generated from F's own protocol definitions rather than hand-written and perpetually stale.
  • Snapshot-based execution. Coverage-guided fuzzing with fast snapshot restore — the technique behind Nyx, which restores thousands of times per second and found 44 bugs with 22 CVEs in hypervisors. F's content-addressed storage makes snapshot and restore natural rather than bolted on.
  • The hostile-peer harness. The highest-value target in the system, and specific to F's threat model. Drive the ring from a deliberately malicious peer: impossible cursor values, entries mutated between validation and use, epochs that jump backwards, a producer that dies mid-claim. This is the direct executable test of the survival rules in the implementation companion, and those rules are load-bearing for the whole imported-driver safety argument.
  • Differential fuzzing against Linux. Run the same imported driver in F's shimmed component and under Linux, feed both identical device-level input, and compare. Divergence is either a shim bug or a genuine behavioural difference worth understanding — and this is the only practical way to gain confidence in a shim implementing an API surface you do not control.
Coverage instrumentation from milestone M0. Fuzzing without coverage feedback is close to worthless, and adding instrumentation to a mature kernel is painful. A KCOV-equivalent that reports executed edges costs very little when the kernel is two thousand lines.
09

Layer 5 — performance regression under noise

This is where most projects' measurement discipline quietly collapses, and the failure is statistical rather than technical. Run-to-run noise swamps the signal, so a fixed threshold either misses real regressions or fires constantly until everyone ignores it. Both outcomes end with nobody watching.

The answer is well established and F should simply adopt it: change point detection rather than thresholds. Treat each metric as a time series over commits and detect statistically significant shifts in its distribution — the approach behind Hunter's modified E-divisive means and the CI systems built on it, which exists precisely because it copes with noisy data where thresholds cannot. A 2026 study across 174 manually annotated performance series found that ensemble voting over multiple detectors improves the precision/recall trade-off by around 11% in F-score, so use an ensemble rather than one detector.

Measure the things that are not noisy

  • Instructions retired per operation is nearly noise-free, attributable to a specific change, and survives a hardware upgrade. Wall-clock time is none of those.
  • Joules per operation is the efficiency claim's actual currency, and section 13 makes it non-optional rather than a nice-to-have.
  • Resident bytes per unit of work catches the regressions nobody notices until a memory-constrained machine falls over.
  • Cache misses, TLB misses and branch mispredictions per operation explain why a time changed, which is what turns a red build into a fix.
Record distributions, never summaries. Averaging at collection time destroys information that cannot be recovered, and a benchmark harness that reports a mean while pausing to record it systematically under-reports exactly the stalls the architecture exists to eliminate. Keep full histograms, publish p50, p99 and p99.9, and make the raw distribution part of the artifact a third party can re-analyze.

Layer 6 — hardware in the loop

Some claims cannot be measured from inside the machine making them, and F's headline interface claim is one of them.

The instrument that settles §05 Input-to-photon cannot be measured in software. The software timestamp ends at the moment of GPU submission; everything after that — scanout, panel response, the display's own processing — is invisible to the system and is a substantial fraction of the number a person actually perceives. The honest instrument is a photodiode fixed to the panel and an instrumented input device sharing one timebase, comparing the electrical moment of input against the optical moment of light. Published rigs sample the photodiode at 14-bit resolution over thousands of samples per run. Without this, a claim of "9 ms typical, 14 ms p99" is an estimate of the part of the pipeline F can see, and should be labelled as such.

The rest of the layer:

  • External power measurement for the energy claims. On-chip energy counters are convenient and are also the thing being optimized against; an external meter is what a skeptic will ask for.
  • Performance counters and processor trace for the counter-based metrics in layer 5, and for cycle-level investigation when a change-point detector fires without an obvious cause.
  • Dedicated bare-metal runners. Shared cloud instances cannot produce defensible tail-latency numbers — noisy neighbours, frequency variation and virtualization overhead all live exactly in the p99.9 the architecture claims to fix. A small number of pinned, thermally stable, physically identical machines is worth more than a large elastic fleet.
Budget the rig early, use it late. The photodiode setup is inexpensive — a photodiode, an ADC, an instrumented switch, a shared clock — but it only becomes useful once there is a compositor to measure, at phase 03. Specify it now so the compositor is built with an external timebase it can be correlated against; retrofitting a shared timebase is much harder than designing for one.

Layer 7 — the claims registry

The layer that turns six layers of machinery into the thing section 13 actually asked for. Every claim in every document becomes a machine-readable record, and the documents render their numbers from that record rather than restating them by hand.

One claim, as a record
claim: ring-submit-latency
  statement:  single ring submission, warm cache, beats an
              equivalent syscall by at least 5×
  baseline:   linux-6.x-tuned  // io_uring, sched_ext, hugepages
  workload:   bench/ring_submit.rs
  metrics:    [ns_per_op_p99, instructions_per_op, joules_per_op]
  threshold:  ns_per_op_p99 < 50 AND ratio_vs_baseline > 5.0
  hardware:   runner-class-A          // pinned bare metal
  reproduce:  cargo xtask claim ring-submit-latency
  history:    claims/ring-submit-latency.jsonl
  status:     gating              // fails the build on regression

What that buys, and why it is worth the modest effort:

  • Documents cannot drift from reality. A number in a published document that no longer holds is the fastest way to lose a reader's trust, and it happens to every long-lived project. Generating the numbers from the registry makes drift structurally impossible.
  • A skeptic runs one command. Section 13's reproducibility requirement stops being an aspiration and becomes a script.
  • The baseline is versioned alongside the claim. Which is what stops the tuned-Linux comparison from quietly decaying into a stock-Linux comparison as the baseline configuration ages and nobody re-checks it.
  • Gating is explicit. Some claims gate the build; some are tracked but advisory. Marking which is which prevents the common failure where a broken benchmark gets muted "temporarily" and stays muted.

What to build when

Mapped onto the milestone ladder in the implementation companion. The ordering is not negotiable at the top and is fairly free at the bottom.

M0 — M1

Layer 0, and the lint that enforces it

The Env capability, the seeded scheduler, and a build-failing lint on every forbidden source of nondeterminism. Plus coverage instrumentation, while the kernel is small enough that adding it is trivial. Nothing else on this page is possible if this slips.

M2

The claims registry, with its first entry

Timer jitter p99 is the first real measurement the project produces. Make it the first registry entry rather than a number in a commit message, and the apparatus grows with the system instead of being retrofitted onto it.

M4

Capability negative tests, then Kani on the same properties

The negative suite is already the phase-00 exit criterion. Bounded model checking of the same properties costs little on top and catches the cases the test author did not think of.

M5

Ring litmus tests and weak-memory checking

The moment the ring exists, before anything is built on it. Litmus tests as permanent regression guards; RustMC on the cursor protocol; ARM64 in CI from this point onward.

Phase 01

The simulator and the hostile-peer fuzzer

Device models arrive with the drivers, so the simulator becomes possible exactly when there is something to simulate. The hostile-peer harness lands with the first cross-trust-boundary ring.

Phase 02

Verus on the frame; change-point detection in CI

Proof work needs a frame that has stopped moving. By this point there is enough commit history for change-point detection to have a distribution to reason about.

Phase 03

The photodiode rig

When there is a compositor to measure. Specify the shared timebase before building the compositor, not after.

13

What not to build

  • Do not write a fuzzer. Coverage-guided mutation engines are a mature field. Write the F-specific parts — the SQE generator and the hostile-peer harness — and drive them with an existing engine.
  • Do not write a model checker. RustMC, Loom and Shuttle exist and are the product of years of research. Write litmus tests, not tooling.
  • Do not attempt whole-system proof. seL4's decade for ten thousand lines is the calibration, and section 08 already made this call. Verify the frame and stop.
  • Do not build a CI system. Use an ordinary one and put the intelligence in the claims registry and the detectors.
  • Do not defer the substrate for velocity. The one irreversible decision on this page. Every week of "we will make it deterministic later" makes later cost more, and past some point it simply does not happen — which is the entire reason no mainstream kernel can do what section 02 describes.
The whole document in one line: build layer 0 in the first fortnight, buy or borrow layers 2 through 5, invest original effort only in the simulator, the hostile-peer harness and the claims registry — because those three are the parts that are specific to F, and they are also the three that turn the architecture's promises into things a skeptic can try to break.
